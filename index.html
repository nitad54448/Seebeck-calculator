How it is calculated

  • Output voltage. V = N · S · (T h − T c). The exact relation is the integral V = N ∫T cT h S(T) dT; this page assumes S is constant across the range, so use the value quoted at the mean temperature when the gradient is large.
  • Sign convention. The result is the potential of the hot-side terminal with respect to the cold-side return. Positive S (p-type) makes the hot side positive, negative S (n-type) makes it negative. Swapping the two wires flips the sign, and reversing the gradient flips it too — both are reflected in the result.
  • Thermopiles. N identical couples in series add their EMFs, and so do their resistances — hence R int should be the measured DC resistance of the whole device, not of a single leg.
  • Electrical power. I = V / (R int + R load), P load = I² · R load, and the matched-load maximum P max = V² / (4 · R int).
  • Efficiency. η = ηCarnot · (√(1+ZT̄) − 1) / (√(1+ZT̄) − T c/T h), with ZT̄ evaluated at (T h + T c)/2. This is the ideal single-pair result: it ignores contact resistance and thermal shorting, so real modules land noticeably below it.
  • Cold-junction compensation. A thermocouple only senses a difference. To read an absolute temperature you must know T c (the terminal block or interface-IC temperature) and add it: T measured = T c + V / (N · S), which is exactly what the ΔT mode reports.
  • Open circuit vs. loaded. The EMF computed is the open-circuit voltage. Drawing current pulls the terminal voltage down by I · R int and simultaneously pumps heat back with the Peltier effect, which is why a generator's best operating point is a compromise between voltage and power.
